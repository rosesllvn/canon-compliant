Chapter 164

Lin Zirui's Heart 林子睿的心里话

July 2026

~6 min read

Mother Yang was usually gentle, but when she got angry there wasn't a soul at home who didn't fear her.

The Lin couple, meeting her for the first time, had been scolded so thoroughly they had no idea how to answer.

"By the way, I've just remembered something — if we're to speak of how Shaoyu and Zirui met, we have you two to thank for introducing them."

"Wh — what do you mean?" Mother Lin had no idea what she meant.

"They first met at a matchmaking date. The one meant to go was Lin Le, but Zirui went in her place, and so they met. Right now I'm especially grateful Lin Le didn't go and Zirui did, so they could meet. Much as I disliked my son being with a man at first, I now think Zirui is wonderful, and I'm very fond of him."

The Lin couple had wondered how the two met — never imagining it was that.

Mother Lin remembered. A friend had proposed the match for Lin Le. She had declined at first, not wanting her daughter married so young, but the friend's praise of the man's family and looks had worn her down. When Lin Le threw a fit and refused to go, she'd sent Zirui in her place to explain.

How could she have known the two would meet then?

"Even so, what of it? Zirui, think it through — break up with this man. I'm only looking out for you—" Father Lin hadn't finished when Mother Yang flared.

They still didn't understand—and meant to take her second son-in-law away besides. She slapped the table. "Don't think I don't know how you treated Zirui. After years of coldness, you dare claim you're looking out for him? I call that hypocrisy."

"Mom, don't be angry — your hand must hurt from hitting the table." Lin Zirui rose, went to Mother Yang, and took the hand she'd pressed to the table; it had indeed gone red.

"Dad, Mom — I'd like to talk with them myself."

Mother Yang disagreed. "There's nothing to talk about with them."

"Mom, let Zirui speak with them himself." Yang Shaoyu knew Lin Zirui's mind.

"Yes, dear." Father Yang agreed too.

"All right — call me if anything happens." Mother Yang still looked uneasy.

Lin Zirui smiled. "I will. Don't worry, Mom."

Watching, Mother Lin felt the sourness again — she'd never been this close to Zirui, and here he was so concerned for that woman. What a waste, all going to her.

Lin Zirui turned to his parents. "Let's go next door and talk."

"All right." Father Lin agreed, leading Mother Lin out first — a private talk was better, away from the overbearing Mother Yang.

The three went to the partition the Lin couple had been in. The moment Lin Zirui sat, he spoke. "First — I will not break up with Shaoyu."

The Lin couple made to speak; Lin Zirui went on. "Shaoyu is the first person I've liked and pursued of my own will. My eye is good — Shaoyu truly is someone worth liking, worth loving.

"Shaoyu is also the first to give me the feeling of being held close in someone's heart, cherished, loved — turning my once-tepid heart hot. Being with him is truly happy; for the first time in my life I'm this happy. Even his family is this warm; they say they're my family, and apart from Eldest and Second Brother, they're the only ones who've given me a family's love."

Mother Lin's heart lurched. "We're your real family…"

"No. You're my birth parents, but for as long as I can remember, I've never felt your love—or even your concern. I studied hard, hoping for praise that never came. Knowing you doted on my little sister, I tried to be a good brother and cherish her, hoping you'd call me a good boy and hold me. As I grew older, I understood that affection couldn't be forced, and I stopped trying to win you over. Still, no parent ever picked me up from school. At parent meetings, everyone else's parents came, but mine never did. That pain has stayed with me no matter how old I've grown. Yet I never imagined my own birth parents could chill my heart so completely. The moment you learned I loved a man, you said you had no such child. That was when I gave up all hope in you."

The Lin couple listened in silence, a thing called guilt, shame, welling up in them. How had they been so muddled? Zirui was their child, yet for twenty-odd years they'd treated him like a stranger.

"Whatever you say, I'll be with Shaoyu. Whether you agree, whether you mean to sever the parent-child bond — my decision won't change. I've said my piece. I'll go now." Lin Zirui stood, bowed to them, and walked out without looking back.

The Lin couple hadn't the face to call him back, still less to face him.

They sat in the partition a long while, and it was full dark when they got home.

The moment she was home Mother Lin went to the door of Lin Zirui's room, wanting to see the room he'd once lived in.

Sitting on his bed, she touched the sheets — cold, like the room itself, its owner long gone, gone chill.

When Lin Le was born, Lin Zirui had been only a few years old. Mother Lin had never stopped to remember that her youngest son was still a small child himself. She and Father Lin had been so overjoyed by their daughter, so determined to give Lin Le everything, that they had denied Zirui even a scrap of parental love or concern.

Her face dimmed, remembering how the eldest and second brothers always called Lin Zirui "Xiao Rui, Xiao Rui" so tenderly, while she, his mother, had never used so affectionate a name — had rarely even said "Zirui."

As Zirui grew, her attention turned all the more to Lin Le, fearing she'd be wronged at school, fearing she'd date — yet she'd never once attended a parent meeting of Zirui's.

Not once.

Zirui said it was a pain he couldn't forget.

Thinking of it, Mother Lin grieved, feeling she'd truly wronged Zirui.

The door opened; Father Lin came in and sat beside her. "We wronged Zirui — wronged our child. I never thought I could be so heartless, so selfish. That Yang woman wasn't wrong to scold us."

Mother Lin bowed her head in shame, then noticed a wooden box on the lowest shelf of the bookcase by the bed. Curious, she took it down and opened it — inside, a key.

"What's that?" Father Lin looked.

"A key to something, I suppose."

He looked around and saw a large wooden box on the bookcase's top shelf.

He brought it down and opened it with the key. Inside were Lin Zirui's childhood belongings and several handwritten notes. One small bottle stood out. Mother Lin opened it and drew out a rolled slip of paper. The crooked characters had plainly been written when Zirui was very young: I hope Mom and Dad will hug me, just a little.

Beside it, a drawn smiley face.

Mother Lin's nose stung; tears fell, and gripping the slip she broke into sobs. She regretted it so — regretted not treating Zirui well, not caring for him more, not cherishing him.

Had she only been good to Zirui, he would have cared for her too, and his heart would never have gone cold.

Father Lin held her, and the two repented together.
